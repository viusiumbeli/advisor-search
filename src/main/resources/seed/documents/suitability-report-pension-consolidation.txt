Introduction and scope of this report

Thank you for meeting with me at our Queen Square office on 7 July 2026 and again on 28 July 2026. This report sets out my recommendation in relation to the three personal pension arrangements you built up alongside your teaching career, and explains the reasoning behind it. It should be read together with the Key Features Document and illustration for the Quillbourne Platform SIPP, which are enclosed.

The scope of my advice is limited to these three arrangements and the question of how best to structure them to provide flexible retirement income. Your defined benefit pension from your teaching career, which is already in payment, is not part of this advice and I have not recommended any change to it. If any information recorded here is inaccurate, please tell me before proceeding, as my recommendation relies on it.


Your circumstances

You are 63, born on 4 March 1963, divorced, in good health and a non-smoker. You retired in August 2024 after thirty-one years as a secondary school teacher, latterly as head of history at a school in north Bristol. You own your home in Redland outright, valued at approximately £485,000, and you have no debts.

Your guaranteed retirement income is a defined benefit teacher's pension of £21,408 a year gross, which increases each April in line with inflation. Your State Pension is due from March 2030, when you reach 67, and your April 2026 forecast estimates it at £12,100 a year in today's terms. You hold £9,500 in an easy access savings account and £16,800 in a cash ISA, which together form your emergency fund.

We recorded your essential expenditure at approximately £1,650 a month (£19,800 a year), covering household bills, food, insurance and running your car. Your teacher's pension covers this almost entirely after tax, and from 2030 your guaranteed income will exceed your essential spending by a comfortable margin. You have no financial dependants. Your daughter, Céleste, aged 34, lives in Cardiff and is the intended beneficiary of your pension death benefits; a fresh nomination form is enclosed for the new plan.


Your objectives and timescale

You told me your objectives, in order of priority, are as follows.

First, you want to draw a flexible income of around £450 a month from your personal pensions to fund travel, your season ticket at the county ground and other discretionary spending, without being locked into an annuity at today's rates.

Second, you want to release a tax-free lump sum of £18,000 within the next three months to replace the roof of your garden studio and to pay for a planned trip to Japan in spring 2027.

Third, you want to simplify your affairs. You currently receive statements from three providers with different valuation dates, and you described managing them as "a part-time job I didn't apply for".

Your investment timescale is long. Although withdrawals begin now, the fund must support drawdown for potentially thirty years or more, and money you do not spend may ultimately pass to Céleste.


Attitude to risk and capacity for loss

You completed our risk questionnaire on 21 July 2026 and scored 5 on our scale of 1 to 10, which we describe as Balanced. We discussed the result on 28 July 2026 and you confirmed it feels right: you accept that values will fall as well as rise, you would be uncomfortable with a fall of more than about a quarter of your fund in a single year, and you understand that holding only cash would see inflation erode your spending power over a thirty-year retirement.

Your capacity for loss is moderate to good in respect of these funds. Because your essential expenditure is met by guaranteed income, a fall in the value of your invested pension would oblige you to trim discretionary spending rather than threaten your standard of living. You could suspend withdrawals entirely for a period if markets fell sharply, and you confirmed you would be willing to do so. I have therefore assessed a Balanced portfolio as consistent with both your willingness and your ability to take risk.


Your existing arrangements

You hold three deferred personal pension arrangements. Values are as at 31 July 2026 and have been confirmed in writing by each provider.

Harleston Mutual Personal Pension, plan number HMP/4471820. Value £86,412, invested wholly in the Harleston Managed Growth Fund. The annual management charge is 1.25%. There is no exit penalty, no protected tax-free cash and no guarantees. The plan does not offer flexi-access drawdown: to draw income you would have to buy an annuity or transfer elsewhere in any event.

Bryncastell Life Retirement Account, policy number BL-88-306142. Value £47,286, invested in the Bryncastell Balanced Managed Fund. Charges are 1.42% a year plus a £52 annual policy fee. Bryncastell Life confirmed on 15 July 2026 that no exit charge applies. There are no safeguarded benefits, and the policy does not support drawdown or partial withdrawals.

Pelham & Wick Provident With-Profits Retirement Plan, policy number PW/1957/60233. Current value £31,847 including attaching bonuses. This policy carries a guaranteed annuity rate of 9.1% at age 65. On current figures that guarantee would secure a level single-life annuity of approximately £2,898 a year for life from March 2028, against roughly £1,783 a year (about 5.6%) on the open market today. This is a valuable safeguarded benefit that would be lost irrevocably on transfer. A market value reduction may also apply to transfers made other than at the policy anniversary.


Why I recommend consolidation of the first two plans

I recommend that you transfer the Harleston Mutual and Bryncastell Life plans, together worth £133,698, into a single self-invested personal pension. My reasons are as follows.

Neither plan can pay you a flexible retirement income. Both are accumulation-only contracts: your stated objective of monthly drawdown withdrawals and an immediate tax-free lump sum simply cannot be met within them, so a transfer of some kind is unavoidable if your objectives are to be achieved.

Consolidation reduces your ongoing charges. The two plans currently cost £1,804 a year (about 1.35% of their combined value). The recommended arrangement, including my ongoing advice fee, will cost £1,537 a year (1.15%), a saving of about £267 a year at current values.

A single plan is simpler to manage, to review and to draw from. One provider, one valuation, one nomination of beneficiaries, and one coherent 60/40 portfolio rather than two overlapping managed funds with dated investment approaches and no common strategy.

Finally, both existing funds are poorly matched to your risk profile: the Harleston fund has held around 80% in equities in recent factsheets, above your Balanced tolerance, while the Bryncastell fund holds a large legacy allocation to UK commercial property that has been suspended from dealing twice since 2020.


What you would be giving up

In transferring the Harleston Mutual and Bryncastell Life plans you give up nothing of guaranteed value, as neither has safeguarded benefits, loyalty bonuses or protected tax-free cash. You will, however, be out of the market for the transfer period, typically ten to fifteen working days per plan, during which a market rise would not benefit you. Both ceding providers have confirmed the transfers can be made through the industry electronic transfer service.

The position on the Pelham & Wick policy is entirely different, and I want to be unambiguous about it.


The Pelham & Wick plan: I recommend you do NOT transfer it

Although you initially asked me to consolidate all three pots, I recommend that the Pelham & Wick with-profits policy is retained until age 65 and its guaranteed annuity rate exercised at that point. The guarantee is worth roughly 60% more income for life than the open market would provide, it is underpinned regardless of investment conditions, and an extra £2,898 a year of guaranteed, non-investment-linked income sits well alongside your teacher's pension in covering your core spending as you age. Transferring this policy into the SIPP would sacrifice that guarantee for the sake of tidiness, and in my professional judgement the loss would clearly outweigh the convenience. Because the policy value exceeds £30,000 and carries safeguarded benefits, regulated advice is required before any transfer; my advice is that no such transfer should take place. You have accepted this recommendation.

The modest disadvantage is that you will continue to receive one separate annual statement until 2028, and the annuity purchased under the guarantee will be level and single-life, so its purchasing power will decline with inflation. We weighed this and you agreed the guarantee is worth keeping.

The recommended portfolio

The blended fund cost is 0.12% a year. The portfolio will be rebalanced automatically each February and August, and reviewed with you annually. Historic modelling of this asset mix suggests a fall of around 25% in a severe year is possible; you confirmed you could tolerate this without abandoning the strategy.


Drawdown strategy and sustainability of withdrawals

I recommend flexi-access drawdown structured as follows. On completion of the transfers, we will crystallise £72,000 of the fund, releasing your required £18,000 tax-free lump sum (25% of the crystallised amount) and moving £54,000 into drawdown. The remaining £61,698 stays uncrystallised, preserving further tax-free cash entitlement for later.

From October 2026 you will draw £450 a month (£5,400 a year) from the drawdown fund. That equates to 4.0% of the combined transfer value, a widely used benchmark for a sustainable starting withdrawal rate. Our cash flow model of 6 August 2026, assuming growth of 4.9% a year net of all charges and inflation of 2.5%, projects the fund lasting beyond age 99 at this rate. Stress testing a 30% market fall in the first year, with no recovery above trend, still sustains withdrawals past age 91. Sustainability improves further in 2028 and 2030 as the Pelham & Wick annuity and then your State Pension begin, at which point you may choose to reduce withdrawals. We will review the withdrawal rate at every annual review and I will recommend a temporary reduction if markets fall materially, to manage sequencing risk.


Tax treatment

The £18,000 lump sum is tax-free and well within your available lump sum allowance of £268,275, of which you have used none. Drawdown income is taxable as earned income through PAYE. Your teacher's pension already uses your £12,570 personal allowance, so the £5,400 annual drawdown income will be taxed at the basic rate, costing £1,080 a year at current rates; your total income remains comfortably below the higher rate threshold. Taking taxable income triggers the money purchase annual allowance, reducing future pension contributions eligible for tax relief to £10,000 a year; as you do not intend to contribute again, this is unlikely to affect you. Under current rules, funds remaining on death before age 75 can normally pass to Céleste free of income tax; after 75 she would pay income tax at her own rate on withdrawals. Tax rules can change and depend on individual circumstances; HMRC practice may alter in future.


Risks and disadvantages

You should weigh the following before proceeding. Investment values can fall as well as rise, and a poor sequence of returns early in drawdown can reduce how long your fund lasts even if average returns are healthy. Drawdown, unlike an annuity, provides no guarantee of income for life, and you bear longevity risk. The 4% starting rate is a guide, not a promise, and may need to be cut. You will be out of the market during the transfers. The £1,671 initial fee means the charge saving alone would take roughly six years to recoup, although cost saving is not the main driver of this advice. Charges on the new plan can change in future. Your money remains protected by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme within its limits, as it is now. Once each transfer completes, your former providers are not obliged to take the money back.
