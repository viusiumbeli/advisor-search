Purpose and basis of this review

This report reviews the investment portfolio we manage on your behalf for the tax year 6 April 2025 to 5 April 2026. It covers valuation and performance, asset allocation and the rebalancing carried out during the year, money paid in and taken out, our commentary on markets and on each holding, the charges you have paid, the position across your tax wrappers, and the cross-border matters that arise from your status as a Norwegian national resident in the United Kingdom. It ends with the actions we agreed at our meeting in your home in Stockbridge on 9 April 2026.

Your objectives are unchanged from last year: growth ahead of inflation over a horizon of ten years or more, with a planned partial retirement from Rowanfield Analytics Ltd at around age 60, and no requirement for regular income from the portfolio at present. Your attitude to risk remains 6 on our 10-point scale, which we reconfirmed by questionnaire on 2 April 2026, and nothing in your circumstances suggests that a lower or higher risk level would now be more suitable.

Valuation and performance

The portfolio was valued at 1,284,930 pounds at close on 5 April 2026, against 1,187,400 pounds at the start of the year. After allowing for the 30,000 pounds you added and the 30,000 pounds you withdrew (set out below), the investment return for the year was 8.2 per cent net of all fund charges and our fees.

Performance is measured against the composite benchmark we agreed in 2023: 40 per cent global developed-market equities in sterling terms, 15 per cent UK equities, 30 per cent sterling investment-grade bonds, 7.5 per cent global listed infrastructure and 7.5 per cent cash at the SONIA rate. The composite returned 7.4 per cent over the year, so the portfolio outperformed by 0.8 percentage points. Over the three years to 5 April 2026 the portfolio has returned 6.1 per cent a year against 5.7 per cent for the composite. We regard three years as the minimum sensible period over which to judge results, and on that basis the portfolio is doing what it was built to do, though we would caution as always that a single year of outperformance carries little information on its own.

By wrapper, the year-end values were: Individual Savings Account 286,140 pounds; Self-Invested Personal Pension 563,870 pounds; general investment account 434,920 pounds.

Asset allocation, drift and rebalancing

Strong equity markets through the summer and autumn of 2025 pushed the portfolio away from target. At our interim check on 21 November 2025 global equities stood at 44.8 per cent against a 40 per cent target, UK equities at 15.6 per cent against 15 per cent, fixed income at 26.2 per cent against 30 per cent, listed infrastructure at 6.1 per cent against 7.5 per cent, and cash at 7.3 per cent against 7.5 per cent. Our policy is to rebalance when any major asset class drifts more than 4 percentage points from target, and global equities breached that band.

On 4 December 2025 we sold 38,000 pounds of the Halloway Global Leaders Fund and directed the proceeds into the Saltire Sterling Corporate Bond Fund and short-dated gilts held directly. A smaller adjustment on 12 February 2026 moved a further 11,000 pounds from UK equities into cash to restore the liquidity reserve after your October withdrawal. At the year end all asset classes were within 1.5 percentage points of target. Where possible we carried out the sales inside the ISA and SIPP so as not to add to the capital gains position in the general account.

You made a single personal contribution to your SIPP of 8,000 pounds net on 19 June 2025, grossed up to 10,000 pounds with basic-rate relief; as a higher-rate taxpayer you can claim a further 2,000 pounds through your self assessment return for 2025/26, and we would ask you to confirm to us once that claim has been made.

You withdrew 30,000 pounds from the general investment account on 14 October 2025 to fund the renovation of the Stockbridge property. Market commentary

The year rewarded patience rather than cleverness. The Bank of England reduced Bank Rate in three steps from 4.5 per cent to 3.75 per cent between May 2025 and February 2026, and the debate through the year was less about the direction of rates than about the pace, with services inflation proving stickier than the headline figures suggested. Ten-year gilt yields, which began the year near 4.6 per cent, ended it close to 4.2 per cent, though not before an uncomfortable episode in September 2025 when concerns over the autumn fiscal statement pushed long yields sharply higher for several weeks. Holders of long-dated gilts had a volatile year; our preference for shorter maturities meant the portfolio was largely insulated from it.

Equity markets made good progress, but the character of the advance changed. The narrow leadership of a handful of large American technology companies, which dominated 2024, gave way from roughly mid-2025 to a broader market in which industrials, financials and, notably, listed infrastructure and utilities participated. UK equities had one of their better years relative to global peers, helped by takeover activity among mid-sized companies and by a dividend yield that looked increasingly attractive as rates fell. We would resist reading too much into one year, but the broadening of returns is healthy, and it modestly favoured the portfolio's tilt away from the largest index names.

Fixed income earned its keep for the first time in several years. Sterling investment-grade credit returned close to 6 per cent as spreads tightened and coupons compounded, and the reinvestment of maturing short gilts at yields above 4 per cent for much of the year was quietly valuable.

Sterling strengthened from around 1.29 to about 1.35 against the US dollar over the year, which trimmed the sterling value of unhedged American assets by several percentage points; against the Norwegian krone it was broadly flat, drifting between 13.2 and 13.8 kroner. The dollar effect is worth understanding rather than lamenting: currency moves of this size wash through a global portfolio in both directions over time, and we do not hedge developed-market equity exposure as a matter of policy, because the cost is certain and the benefit is not.

Individual holdings

Halloway Global Leaders Fund (28.4 per cent of the portfolio at year end). Returned 12.4 per cent. The fund's discipline of trimming positions that exceed 5 per cent of its assets served it well when market leadership broadened, and stock selection in industrials and healthcare added value. This remains the core global equity holding and we made no change beyond the December rebalancing sale.

Ardenmuir UK Equity Income Fund (13.1 per cent). Returned 9.6 per cent with a distribution yield of 4.1 per cent. Two of its mid-cap holdings received takeover approaches during the year. The manager's aversion to the highly indebted end of the UK market has cost performance in racier years but was rewarded in this one. Retained without change.

Saltire Sterling Corporate Bond Fund (18.2 per cent). Returned 5.9 per cent. Duration was kept slightly short of the index through the September gilt episode, which protected capital. The running yield at year end was 5.2 per cent. We added to this fund in December as part of the rebalancing.

Kestrel Global Infrastructure Fund (4.2 per cent at year end, down from 7.5 per cent). Returned 2.1 per cent for the period we held the full position. The asset class itself performed respectably, but we reduced this holding by roughly half in early December because Marius Kleiven, the fund's lead manager since its launch in 2017 and in our judgement the principal reason for holding it, left the management company in November 2025. The two co-managers who have taken over are capable, but their record is short and the fund's approach has been unusually dependent on one individual's project-level assessment of regulated utility and transport assets. Rather than sell outright, we retained a reduced position and will give the new team four quarters to demonstrate continuity of process; the proceeds of the reduction went into the December rebalancing. We will report on this specifically at the next review.

Meridian Emerging Markets Opportunities Fund (6.8 per cent). Returned minus 3.8 per cent, the portfolio's clearest disappointment. The fund's underweight to the largest Asian index constituents, which drove strong returns in 2023 and 2024, worked against it this year, and currency weakness in two of its larger Latin American positions compounded the shortfall. We considered selling and decided against it. The manager is doing what the mandate says on the tin; the holdings trade at a meaningful discount to their developed-market peers; and selling a disciplined manager immediately after a poor year is, in our experience, how investors convert temporary underperformance into permanent loss. Retained, but kept on our internal watch list.

Wynborne Absolute Return Fund (sold in full, January 2026). Returned 0.9 per cent over the portion of the year we held it, against a cash return of approximately 4.7 per cent. This fund was bought in 2022 to dampen volatility when cash yielded almost nothing. That rationale has expired: cash and short gilts now provide a better return with less complexity and a fee of a fraction of Wynborne's 0.92 per cent. After three years in which the fund failed to beat its own cash-plus objective, we sold the remaining units on 15 January 2026 and hold the proceeds in short-dated gilts and the platform cash facility pending the actions agreed below.

The balance of the portfolio, around 8 per cent, is held in short-dated gilts and cash.

Cross-border considerations

As a Norwegian national who has been resident in the United Kingdom since 2018, you remain UK tax resident under the statutory residence test, and your worldwide income and gains are within the scope of UK tax. Three specific matters are worth recording.

First, the legacy holding of Norwegian listed shares you retained on emigration, currently worth about 41,000 pounds and held in the general account, continues to suffer Norwegian withholding tax on dividends at 25 per cent at source. Under the UK-Norway double taxation convention the rate should be 15 per cent, and credit for that 15 per cent is available against your UK dividend tax liability. We submitted a reclaim to the Norwegian tax authorities on 3 February 2026 for the excess withheld in 2024 and 2025, amounting to roughly 3,900 kroner; refunds have historically taken six to nine months.

Second, the Norwegian exit tax that attached to gains latent at your emigration in 2018 lapsed under the five-year rule then in force, so a disposal of the legacy shares no longer creates a Norwegian liability. Anyone emigrating from Norway after late 2024 faces far stricter rules, but your position is grandfathered. This removes the main historical objection to selling those shares, and consolidation into the main portfolio is now primarily a UK capital gains question.

Third, your SIPP. Should you return to Norway in retirement, which you have said is possible but not planned, the convention gives the state of residence primary taxing rights over pension income, so drawdown payments would generally be taxable in Norway rather than the UK, and the interaction with the 25 per cent UK tax-free lump sum needs care, since Norway does not recognise that exemption. Nothing needs to be decided now, but we recommend that no tax-free cash be taken until your country of retirement is settled, and we have noted this on your file.
